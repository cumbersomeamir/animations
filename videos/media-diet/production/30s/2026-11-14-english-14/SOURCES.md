**Exact topic:** "Scale-rich network-based metamaterials" — Csaba Both, Andrew Yen-Jong Chen, Ting-Ting Gao, Niek Mooij, Mohammad
Charara, Jayna Wadhwa, Carlos M. Portela, Albert-László Barabási (MIT, Northeastern University, Utrecht University). Published in
Nature on 11 Nov 2026; MIT News release the same day. 3D-printed polymer samples whose beams span many lengths and thicknesses
(a network, not a repeated unit cell) were more resilient to compression and dropped weights than conventional lattice and
honeycomb metamaterials.

**Status:** peer-reviewed lab research with 3D-printed test samples. Not a product; proposed applications (helmets, armour, acoustic
lenses) are prospects, not claims.

## Primary / official sources (read 14 Nov 2026)
1. Nature — "Scale-rich network-based metamaterials", published 2026-11-11, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11191-2
   Abstract: mechanical metamaterials "remain defined by the homogeneity of their unit cells"; scale-rich metamaterials achieve
   "orders-of-magnitude heterogeneity in ligament lengths, thicknesses and connectivity", are "governed by only two parameters", and
   show "delocalized nonlinear deformation with high energy absorption, enhanced energy dissipation under dynamic loading" and
   programmable elastic-wave control.
2. MIT News — "Inspired by networks found in nature, engineers design stronger, more resilient 'metamaterials'", 11 Nov 2026,
   https://news.mit.edu/2026/engineers-design-stronger-resilient-nature-inspired-metamaterials-1111
   - Ordered metamaterials repeat a single unit cell; "if one region of the material is deformed, the surrounding, similar unit cells
     will respond and deform in kind" — the "localization effect" (Portela).
   - Design algorithm: a point at a random coordinate and a line at a random angle across the domain; each further line runs until it
     reaches the boundary or an earlier line; lines get progressively shorter and thinner; two parameters (starting thickness and how
     fast it decreases) tune stiffness.
   - Samples ≈ credit-card-sized squares, 3D-printed polymer, beams "from microns to centimeters".
   - Tests: dropping a large weight and squeezing with increasing force, alongside conventional lattice and honeycomb samples;
     "The network-inspired samples … were more resilient to compression and impacts."
   - "If one part fails, there is a strong web of structures around it that keeps the entire material from failing catastrophically.
     It's more efficient at distributing damage and absorbing energy." (Portela)
   - A pomelo-rind-inspired, density-graded version cushioned a fragile embedded object (not shown in the video).
3. Penn State Engineering Science & Mechanics — Euler buckling tutorial: critical load P_cr = π²EI/L²
   https://sites.esm.psu.edu/courses/emch213d/tutorials/animations/buckling.php  (cross-check: Mechanics Reference, https://mechref.org/sol/buckling)
