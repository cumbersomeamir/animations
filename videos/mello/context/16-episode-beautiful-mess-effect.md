## Research (sources checked 2026-11-06)
1. **Bruk, A., Scholl, S. G., & Bless, H. (2018).** Beautiful mess effect: Self–other differences in evaluation of showing vulnerability. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 115*(2), 192–205. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000120 (abstract read via PubMed, PMID 30024240)
   - "Confessing romantic feelings, asking for help, or taking responsibility for a mistake constitute just a few examples of situations that require showing one's vulnerability."
   - "A total of seven studies demonstrate the predicted self-other differences in the evaluation of showing vulnerability in various situations."
   - The explanation is construal level. One's own vulnerable situation is represented concretely, with the focus on the negatives. Others' situations are represented abstractly, with more focus on the positives.
2. **Bruk, A., Scholl, S. G., & Bless, H. (2022).** You and I both: Self-compassion reduces self–other differences in evaluation of showing vulnerability. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 48*(7), 1054–1067. https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672211031080 (open access, full text read via PMC9178778)
   - "People tend to be overly critical of their own displays of vulnerability, whereas observers evaluate others' showing of vulnerability rather positively (beautiful mess effect)."
   - Study 3 used "confessing a mistake to one's boss (Bruk et al., 2018)". Participants who imagined another person admitting a mistake evaluated it more positively than those imagining themselves: M = 4.92 vs 4.65 on a 1–7 scale, F(1, 92) = 13.99, p < .001.
   - **Magnitude:** reliable but modest. The script therefore says "more kindly", never "much more", and the on-screen sliders are schematic (direction only, no scale).

How the script uses them:
- "people judged someone else admitting a mistake more kindly than themselves" summarises 2018 (seven studies, mistake scenario included) and 2022 Study 3 (the mistake scenario).
- "Researchers call it the beautiful mess effect" uses the authors' term.
- "If a colleague owned this mistake, what would I think of them?" is the practical perspective switch: it takes the observer's view, which the studies show is the kinder one.
- "So own it in one plain line, and send the fix" is the demonstrated action. It is advice, not a claimed outcome.

Not claimed: how any particular colleague will react, anxiety relief or treatment, effect sizes, or any app feature. The colleague's "Thanks for flagging!" reply is an illustration.
