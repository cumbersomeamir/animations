## Research (sources checked 2026-11-23)
1. **Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006).** Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science, 17*(3), 249–255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x (PMID 16507066; abstract read; full text not openly available — the authors' lab server timed out, and no workaround was attempted)
   - "students studied prose passages and took one or three immediate free-recall tests, without feedback, or restudied the material the same number of times as the students who received tests. Students then took a final retention test 5 min, 2 days, or 1 week later."
   - "When the final test was given after 5 min, repeated studying improved recall relative to repeated testing. However, on the delayed tests, prior testing produced substantially greater retention than studying, even though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material."
2. **Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L., III (2008).** The critical importance of retrieval for learning. *Science, 319*(5865), 966–968. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408 (PMID 18276894; abstract)
   - "Repeated studying after learning had no effect on delayed recall, but repeated testing produced a large positive effect. In addition, students' predictions of their performance were uncorrelated with actual performance."
3. **Rowland, C. A. (2014).** The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: A meta-analytic review of the testing effect. *Psychological Bulletin, 140*(6), 1432–1463. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559 (PMID 25150680; abstract)
   - "initial recall tests yielding larger testing benefits than recognition tests". Sigayret et al. 2026 report its overall estimate as g = 0.50.
4. **Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013).** Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14*(1), 4–58. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266 (PMID 26173288; abstract)
   - "Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility assessments"; "rereading" received "a low utility assessment"; "Most students report rereading and highlighting, yet these techniques do not consistently boost students' performance".
5. **Limit: Sigayret, K., Parmentier, J.-F., & Silvestre, F. (2026).** Testing the testing effect on Prolific: When retrieval practice fails to boost learning. *Frontiers in Psychology, 17*, 1727423. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1727423 (PMID 41694023; PMC12894256, full text read)
   - "No significant differences were found between groups at delayed test", in two online experiments with feedback and delayed tests. The authors attribute this mainly to low engagement online.
   - Their literature summary: meta-analytic effect sizes "between d = 0.55 and d = 0.88 (Phelps, 2012), g = 0.50 (Rowland, 2014), and g = 0.61 to g = 0.70 (Adesope et al., 2017)"; the effect "tends to be stronger … when corrective feedback or performance incentives are included"; "the testing effect is not invariant across learners, tasks, or contexts".

How the script uses them (measured / reported / advice / illustrative):
- **Hook:** "Still rereading your notes?" (Dunlosky 2013: most students report rereading.)
- **Measured (Roediger & Karpicke 2006):**
  - "In one study, students reread a passage, or tried recalling it." (restudy vs free-recall tests on prose passages);
  - "Five minutes later, rereading looked better. A week later, recalling did better."
- **Reported (2006):** "Rereading felt more certain, but recall lasted." ("repeated studying increased students' confidence"; "prior testing produced substantially greater retention" on delayed tests.)
- **Meta-analytic support plus limit:** "Many studies agree, though not all." (Rowland 2014; Adesope 2017; Dunlosky 2013. Not all: e.g. Sigayret et al. 2026 online; the effect varies by learner, task and context.)
- **Advice (no promised outcome):** "So close your notes, write what you remember, then check what you missed." Free recall followed by feedback; feedback tends to strengthen the effect (Sigayret 2026 summary).
- **Not claimed:** exact percentages (the full paper was not read), brain mechanisms, or guaranteed grades.
- **Illustrative:**
  - the lilac lines lifting off the pages;
  - the lilac vs gold memory strips;
  - the card's tiles and bars (direction only, no values);
  - the notebook, the recall card's lines and the filled gap.
