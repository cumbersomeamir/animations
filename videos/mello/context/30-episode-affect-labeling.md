## Research (sources checked 2026-11-17; PubMed abstracts)
1. **Lieberman, M. D., Inagaki, T. K., Tabibnia, G., & Crockett, M. J. (2011).** Subjective responses to emotional stimuli during labeling, reappraisal, and distraction. *Emotion, 11*(3), 468–480. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023503 (PMID 21534661; PMC3444304)
   - "In study one, self-reported distress was lower during affect labeling, compared to passive watching, of negative emotional pictures. Studies two and three added reappraisal and distraction conditions, respectively. Affect labeling showed similar effects on self-reported distress as both of these intentional emotion regulation strategies."
   - "participant predictions about the effects of affect labeling suggest that unlike reappraisal and distraction, people do not believe affect labeling to be an effective emotion regulation strategy. Even after having the experience of affect labels leading to lower distress, participants still predicted that affect labeling would increase distress in the future."
   - Study 4: with positive pictures, labeling also dampened pleasure. So it dampens feelings in general; it is not specific to negative ones.
2. **Lieberman, M. D., Eisenberger, N. I., Crockett, M. J., Tom, S. M., Pfeifer, J. H., & Way, B. M. (2007).** Putting feelings into words: Affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli. *Psychological Science, 18*(5), 421–428. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x (PMID 17576282)
   - "affect labeling, relative to other forms of encoding, diminished the response of the amygdala and other limbic regions to negative emotional images."
3. **Background, not narrated:** Kircanski, K., Lieberman, M. D., & Craske, M. G. (2012). Feelings into words: Contributions of language to exposure therapy. *Psychological Science, 23*(10), 1086–1091 (PMID 22902568).
   - Spider-fearful participants: the affect-labeling group had lower skin conductance at 1 week, but did not differ in self-reported fear. The results are mixed, so this study is not used for a claim.

How the script uses them (measured / reported / advice / illustrative):
- **Reported (2011):** "Many people expect it to make things worse." Participants predicted labeling would increase distress.
- **Measured (2011, Studies 1–3):** "But in studies, putting feelings into words while viewing upsetting images lowered reported distress. It worked about as well as reframing or distraction."
- **Measured (2007):** "Brain scans showed less amygdala activity, too." Relative to other ways of processing the images.
- **Advice (no promised outcome):** "So when a tense message lands, pause and name it: I feel anxious."
- **Qualification:** "The feeling stays, but often a little softer." Labeling dampened responses; it did not remove them, and Kircanski 2012 found no change in self-reported fear.
