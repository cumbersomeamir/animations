## Research (sources checked 2026-11-05)
1. **Buehler, R. (2019, May 30).** "The Planning Fallacy: An Inside View." SPSP *Character & Context* blog, written by the researcher who led the core studies. Full text read: https://spsp.org/news-center/blog/buehler-planning-fallacy
   - "The planning fallacy refers to an optimistic prediction bias in which people underestimate the time it will take them to complete a task, despite knowing that similar tasks have typically taken them much longer in the past."
   - "It appears for small tasks like daily household chores (such as cleaning), as well as for large-scale infrastructure projects."
   - "The problem is that events usually don't unfold exactly as people imagine. Even when people create a thoughtful mental scenario in advance, they will likely encounter unexpected obstacles, delays, and interruptions."
   - "People could usually make more realistic predictions by taking an 'outside view' in which they base their predictions on their prior experiences."
   - Reference class forecasting compares "the current project with similar projects the person has completed in the past". It "has been shown to improve forecast accuracy for large-scale construction projects and may have similar benefits among people trying to make predictions about personal projects".
2. **Roy, M. M., Christenfeld, N. J. S., & McKenzie, C. R. M. (2005).** Underestimating the duration of future events: Memory incorrectly used or memory bias? *Psychological Bulletin, 131*(5), 738–756. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.131.5.738
   - Abstract read via PubMed (PMID 16187856): "People base predictions of future duration on their memories of how long past events have taken, but these memories are systematic underestimates of past duration. People appear to underestimate future event duration because they underestimate past event duration."
3. **Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979).** Intuitive prediction: Biases and corrective procedures. *TIMS Studies in Management Science, 12*, 313–327. This paper coined the term. It is cited through Buehler (2019); I did not read it directly.
4. **Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994).** Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. *JPSP, 67*(3), 366–381. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366
   - The core empirical paper. The only open copy failed TLS through the session proxy (not bypassed), and the publisher's copy is closed access.
   - So **no study numbers are spoken**. The widely quoted thesis figures (about 34 vs 56 days) were left out because I could not check them in the primary text.

How the script uses them:
- "हम आने वाले काम को परफ़ेक्ट सोचते हैं" / "बीच में बहुत कुछ आ जाता है" (we imagine the task ahead as perfect / a lot comes up along the way) come from Buehler (2019): events don't unfold as imagined, and interruptions follow.
- "अंदाज़े की जगह, पिछली बार का असली समय देखिए" (instead of guessing, look at last time's actual time) is the outside view in Buehler (2019): base the prediction on prior experience with similar tasks.
- "काम के बाद, असली समय लिख लीजिए" (after the task, write down the actual time) follows Roy et al. (2005): memories of past durations run short, so a written record is a better base than memory.
- The numbers 55 मिनट, 58 मिनट and 6:00 are illustrative example values in the demonstration, not data.

Not claimed: guaranteed accuracy or productivity, any statistic, or that the app has planning or time-tracking features.

## Skill / scene adjustments for future episodes
- **Flat dial props for steep views.** A clock or dial lying face-up reads well from the accepted high over-the-shoulder views. Wedges show plan vs overrun with no text needed.
- **Desk layout for this episode.** Clock at the back-left (x −0.30), mug at the front-left, phone at the front-left, task sheet at the front-centre, notebook at the right (x 0.25, z 0.12). Keep busy props out of the S3 caption band; the clock had to move left for that.
- **Arms vs in-scene text.** With rear-right cameras the right arm enters from the lower-left. Hands holding over a page should rest below its text block, and the other hand should not reach across.
