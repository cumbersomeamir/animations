# Episode brief: "A thought is not automatically a fact" (feedback-01)

Round: 2026-11-02 feedback round. Deliverable: `videos/mello/production/30s/2026-11-02-feedback-round/mello-30s-feedback-01.mp4` (one video only).

## User brief (as given)
- Demonstrate the simple cognitive-defusion wording **"I'm noticing the thought that…"**.
- It is a way of relating to a thought, **not a promise** that anxiety or distress disappears.
- Make the exercise concrete and useful. No personality manipulation, fabricated brain statistics or "master any skill in 20 hours" guarantee.
- Scene: at a desk, a solid thought card reading "I'll mess this up" first blocks the figure's view. On the rephrasing, the card becomes a transparent frame and moves beside the person. The thought stays visible while the person returns attention to a simple task. Cut to a small action (placing one notebook on the desk), then return to the wider calm room.
- The metaphor shows distance from a thought, not deleting negative feelings.
- Five distinct shots, restrained movement, no decorative noise and no stock presenter.

## Research (sources checked 2026-11-02)
Verified facts from the sources:
1. **Luoma, J. B. & Hayes, S. C., "Cognitive Defusion".** In W. T. O'Donohue, J. E. Fisher & S. C. Hayes (Eds.), *Empirically Supported Techniques of Cognitive Behavior Therapy: A Step-by-Step Guide for Clinicians*, Wiley. Author PDF: https://www.personal.kent.edu/~dfresco/CBT_Readings/Luoma_Cognitive_Defusion.pdf
   - Cognitive distancing means seeing thoughts "as hypotheses rather than objective facts about the world".
   - The chapter's table of defusion techniques includes **"I'm having the thought that"** ("Include category labels in descriptions of private events") and **"Just noticing"** ("Use the language of observation (e.g., noticing) when talking about thoughts").
   - Defusion aims to reduce the impact of thoughts "without having first to alter the form, frequency, or situational sensitivity of those thoughts".
   - The episode's wording, "I'm noticing the thought that…", combines these two listed techniques.
2. **Northern Cancer Alliance (NHS), Dr Jenny Johns, Clinical Lead for Psychology, "Acceptance & Commitment Therapy (ACT) in Cancer Care"** (slides, Nov 2023): https://northerncanceralliance.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Improving-Emotional-Wellbeing-for-People-Affected-by-Cancer.pdf
   - "Tool 3 – Defusion – 'Name it to tame it'" gives the example "I notice that I'm having the thought that the cancer might come back".
   - "We don't argue with the thought, or try to change the feeling, we mindfully notice it and 'name it'". "We gently return our mind to the present moment and refocus on living in line with our values."
3. **Supporting source:** "Thought Defusion: cognitive distancing techniques" (TherapistAid worksheet, hosted by Homerton Healthcare NHS FT): https://www.homerton.nhs.uk/download/10-pst-thought-defusion-cognitive-distancing-technipdf.pdf?ver=44267&doc=docm93jijm4n17976.pdf
   - "put the phrase 'I'm having the thought that…' in front of it. This creates distance between you and the thought".
4. **Background:** ACBS page "Cognitive Defusion (Deliteralization)", clinical materials credited to Gifford, Hayes and Strosahl: https://contextualscience.org/da/book/export/html/31
   - Lists "I'm having the thought that…" under "cubbyholing".

Not claimed: any statistic, effect size, guaranteed outcome, symptom reduction, or that the app teaches this technique.

## Script: draft vs final
User draft:
> A thought can sound like a fact: I'll mess this up. Try adding one phrase: I'm noticing the thought that I'll mess this up. The thought is still there, but now you are observing it instead of treating it as a prediction. This technique is called cognitive defusion. You do not have to argue with the thought. Notice it, then choose one small useful action. Take a mindful break with Mello.

Final narration, as recorded:
> A thought can sound like a fact: I'll mess this up. / Try adding one phrase: / I'm noticing the thought that, / I'll mess this up. / The thought is still there, / but now you're observing it, instead of treating it as a prediction. / This is a cognitive defusion technique. / You don't have to argue with the thought. / Notice it, then choose one small, useful action. / Take a mindful break with Mello.

Changes and why:
- "This technique is called cognitive defusion" → **"This is a cognitive defusion technique."** Cognitive defusion is the ACT process, and "I'm having / noticing the thought that…" is one technique within it (Luoma & Hayes list it among several).
- "you are" → "you're" and "do not" → "don't", for natural speech. No meaning change.
- "small useful" → "small, useful": a comma only, for the reading.
- The material qualification sits in the narration itself: "The thought is still there". The video never says the thought or feeling goes away.
- The CTA stays the user's line. It does not claim a feature: the listing describes guided relaxation and calming exercises.
