## Research (sources checked 2026-11-27)
1. **Bonezzi, A., Brendl, C. M., & De Angelis, M. (2011).** Stuck in the middle: The psychophysics of goal pursuit. *Psychological Science, 22*(5), 607–612. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611404899 (PMID 21474842; abstract)
   - "we show that motivation to engage in goal-consistent behavior can be higher when people are either far from or close to the end state and lower when they are about halfway to the end state … this tendency to get 'stuck in the middle.'"
   - "the shape of the goal gradient varies depending on whether an individual monitors progress in terms of distance from the initial state or from the desired end state."
   - **Study details, as described in Emanuel, A. (2019).** Perceived impact as the underpinning mechanism of the end-spurt and U-shape pacing patterns. *Frontiers in Psychology, 10*, 1082. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01082 (PMC6519309, full text read):
     - "People were also found to demonstrate a stuck-in-the-middle pattern when correcting typos through a series of nine articles; finding typos at a higher rate in the beginning and end of the series compared to its middle (Bonezzi et al., 2011; Study 3)."
     - Study 1, a "words in a word" game, showed the same U shape across a nine-word series.
2. **Touré-Tillery, M., & Fishbach, A. (2012).** The end justifies the means, but only in the middle. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 141*(3), 570–583. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025928 (PMID 22004169; abstract)
   - "people are more likely to adhere to their standards at the beginning and end of goal pursuit—and slack in the middle", including "performance standards (e.g., 'cutting corners' on a task)".
3. **Koo, M., & Fishbach, A. (2012).** The small-area hypothesis: Effects of progress monitoring on goal adherence. *Journal of Consumer Research, 39*(3), 493–509. https://doi.org/10.1086/663827 (abstract via Crossref; the full text was not reachable — the author link returned 404)
   - "individuals striving toward a goal end state exhibit greater motivation when their attention is directed to whichever is smaller in size—their accumulated or remaining progress."
   - "at the beginning of goal pursuit, directing attention to accumulated progress increases goal adherence relative to directing attention to remaining progress (e.g., 20% completed is more impactful than 80% remaining). However, with closeness to the goal, directing attention to accumulated progress lessens goal adherence relative to directing attention to remaining progress (e.g., 20% remaining is more impactful than 80% completed; studies 1–2)."
   - "The focus on small areas increases motivation by creating an illusion of fast progress (study 3)."
   - Emanuel 2019 summarises: "people use the nearest reference point for progress-monitoring … the starting point as a reference as they start, and then switch to use the end point as a reference mid-way through the task."
4. **Limits:** no failed replication of the small-area or stuck-in-the-middle findings turned up in my searches (PubMed, Europe PMC, Semantic Scholar; the last was rate-limited). That is "none found", not "none exist".
   - The evidence comes from a small number of studies (some in consumer settings, e.g. loyalty cards).
   - Hence "can sag", "In one study" and "Other studies found"; no promise.

How the script uses them (measured / reported / advice / illustrative):
- **Hook:** "Stuck halfway through?" (a question).
- **Reported (Bonezzi 2011; Touré-Tillery & Fishbach 2012):** "Motivation can sag in the middle of a goal."
- **Measured (Bonezzi 2011, Study 3, per Emanuel 2019):** "In one study, proofreaders caught more typos at the start and end than in the middle."
- **Measured (Koo & Fishbach 2012):**
  - "Other studies found people stuck with goals more when they watched the smaller number." ("goal adherence");
  - "Early on, that's what's done. Near the end, it's what's left." (their 20% examples);
  - "Progress feels faster." (study 3: "an illusion of fast progress").
- **Advice (no promised outcome):** "So at first, count what you've done. Past halfway, count what's left."
- **Not claimed:** effect sizes, a guarantee, or that this works for every goal.
- **Illustrative:**
  - the tube, balls and halfway ring, and the "N done / N left" counts;
  - the card's U-shaped bars over nine page icons (direction only, no values; only the higher-at-ends shape is from the study).
  - The 20% / 80% labels are the paper's own example.
