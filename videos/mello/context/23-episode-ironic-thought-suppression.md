## Research (sources checked 2026-11-13; PubMed abstracts)
1. **Wegner, D. M., Schneider, D. J., Carter, S. R., & White, T. L. (1987).** Paradoxical effects of thought suppression. *JPSP, 53*(1), 5–13. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.53.1.5 (PMID 3612492)
   - Subjects "were asked to try not to think of a white bear, but to ring a bell in case they did … they were unable to suppress the thought as instructed."
   - After suppression, asked to think about the bear, they "showed significantly more tokens of thought about the bear than did subjects who were asked to think about a white bear from the outset."
   - Experiment 2: "subjects given a specific thought to use as a distracter during suppression were less likely to exhibit later preoccupation with the thought to be suppressed."
2. **Wegner, D. M. (1994).** Ironic processes of mental control. *Psychological Review, 101*(1), 34–52. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.101.1.34 (PMID 8121959)
   - A "monitoring process that tests whether the operating process is needed by searching for mental contents inconsistent with the intended state"; under reduced capacity it "may supersede the operating process".
3. **Abramowitz, J. S., Tolin, D. F., & Street, G. P. (2001).** Paradoxical effects of thought suppression: a meta-analysis of controlled studies. *Clinical Psychology Review, 21*(5), 683–703. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0272-7358(00)00057-X (PMID 11434226)
   - "a small to moderate rebound effect of thought suppression that varied in magnitude".
4. **Wang, D. A., Hagger, M. S., & Chatzisarantis, N. L. D. (2020).** Ironic effects of thought suppression: A meta-analysis. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 15*(3), 778–793. https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691619898795 (PMID 32286932)
   - Rebound effects "were consistently detected by previous meta-analyses"; in 31 studies "rebound effects were observed regardless of cognitive load", while immediate enhancement appeared only under load.
