## Research (sources checked 2026-11-14; PubMed abstracts)
1. **Grossmann, I., & Kross, E. (2014).** Exploring Solomon's paradox: Self-distancing eliminates the self-other asymmetry in wise reasoning about close relationships in younger and older adults. *Psychological Science, 25*(8), 1571–1580. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535400 (PMID 24916084)
   - "In three experiments (N = 693), participants displayed wiser reasoning (i.e., recognizing the limits of their knowledge and the importance of compromise and future change, considering other people's perspectives) about another person's problems compared with their own."
   - "Across Studies 2 and 3, instructing individuals to self-distance (rather than self-immerse) eliminated this asymmetry."
2. **Kross, E., Bruehlman-Senecal, E., Park, J., Burson, A., Dougherty, A., Shablack, H., Bremner, R., Moser, J., & Ayduk, O. (2014).** Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism: How you do it matters. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 106*(2), 304–324. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035173 (PMID 24467424)
   - "using non-first-person pronouns and one's own name (rather than first-person pronouns) during introspection enhances self-distancing."
   - "Compared with the first-person group, the non-first-person group performed better according to objective raters in both studies. They also displayed less distress (Studies 2 and 3)." Study 3 was public speaking.

How the script uses them (measured / illustrative):
- **Measured (Grossmann & Kross 2014):** "Across three experiments, people reasoned more wisely about someone else's conflict than their own." "But when they reflected from a distance, the gap disappeared." The abstract says self-distancing "eliminated this asymmetry" in Studies 2 and 3.
- **Name:** "Researchers call it Solomon's paradox." This is the authors' term in the paper's title.
- **Measured (Kross et al. 2014):** "Self-talk using your own name also led to better speeches and less distress." This covers the non-first-person group in the first-impression and public-speaking studies, with performance judged by raters.
- **Application (advice):** "So when you're stuck, ask yourself by name: Alex, what would you tell a friend?" This combines both findings (self-distancing; others' perspective). "Alex" is an example name; it is advice, not a promised result.
- **Illustrative:** the knots and their loosening, the bar lengths on the "wise reasoning" card (direction only; equal bars show "the gap disappeared"), and the "Why am I nervous?" → "Why is Alex nervous?" bubble, which mirrors the first-person vs own-name wording of the studies.
