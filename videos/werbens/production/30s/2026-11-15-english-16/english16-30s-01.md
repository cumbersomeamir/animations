## 2. Topic check against the episode history → decoy covered; fresh alternative chosen
**The decoy effect is already covered.** Episode **#03** (`production/30s/2026-11-02-feedback-round/`, context/14) used an
illustrative coffee menu: small 150 ml Rs90, medium 280 ml Rs155 (decoy), large 300 ml Rs160. The medium is
asymmetrically dominated by the large, which makes the large look like a bargain.

**Chosen instead: the compromise effect (extremeness aversion)**, the same family and the same brief, with a different
mechanism:
- Adding an *extreme* option, here a more expensive Premium tier that most people won't buy, makes the *middle* option more
  attractive because it becomes the compromise.
- No option is dominated. The effect comes from people avoiding extremes, not from a near-identical inferior option.
- The evidence and the lesson differ from #03. The example also differs: made-up subscription plans instead of a cup menu, with
  no prices at all.

Other earlier episodes:
- #09 (anchoring) is about a reference price, not choice shares.
- #13 (choice overload) is about the number of options, not their position.

The hook "THE OPTION YOU WON'T BUY" stays truthful: the extreme option is the one most shoppers skip.

## 7. How the rules were applied (creative decisions)
- **Frame 0 = thumbnail.** Three made-up plan pedestals of rising height on the navy stage: BASIC (ink), PLUS (teal) and a tall
  PREMIUM (mint-white, spotlit). Coins show price level. Shoppers crowd the middle (3 / 8 / 1). The headline reads
  "THE OPTION / YOU WON'T BUY" (Space Grotesk 700; the second line uses the hero gradient), x 120–880. No intro, no fade. The
  headline clears by 0.42 s as the first caption appears.
- **One real question:** "why can an option you skip change your pick?"
  - Build-up: removing and re-adding Premium shows the choices rearranging. The split is 6 / 6, then shoppers hop to Plus.
  - Proof: the comparative-value principle (neighbors), then the measured finding (middle gains, extremes lose).
  - Payoff: the takeaway, then the ethical rule.
- **One visual idea at a time:**
  1. Premium sinks into the floor ("MADE-UP PLANS"); shoppers re-split evenly.
  2. Premium rises again; shoppers hop over to Plus.
  3. Basic and Premium lean in toward Plus on "neighbors"; Plus pulses on "alone".
  4. Lateral move to the proof diagram (EXTREME / MIDDLE / EXTREME): the middle bar rises ("MIDDLE GAINS"), then the extremes
     sink ("EXTREMES LOSE").
  5. Back to the plans: a ripple runs through the menu.
  6. One real customer steps onto each tier ("REAL CUSTOMERS").
  7. On "bait" the shoppers shrink away and Plus sinks into the floor; Basic and Premium slide apart as the logo tile pops up
     in Plus's place.
- **Labels:** MADE-UP PLANS / MIDDLE GAINS / EXTREMES LOSE / REAL CUSTOMERS. All large and brief, one at a time, y ≥ 574.
- **Small in-world plates:** BASIC, PLUS, PREMIUM, EXTREME, MIDDLE.
- **Excluded:** no prices, no currency, no source chips, no decorative lines.
- **Reusable lessons:**
  1. Put name plates near the top of pedestals so pawns in front never cover them.
  2. Place the proof set close enough that mid-move frames show both sets.
  3. Centre props must leave the stage too: they sink or shrink for the CTA.
  4. Check homophones and spellings that break caption alignment ("pricier", "neighbours").
  5. Keep in-world text plates inside x ≈ 100–900 as well: the first bar framing put the right EXTREME chip at x 920–1060,
     y ≈ 1390, under the TikTok/Reels icon rail. The fix was narrower bars and chips plus a wider camera.
  6. Clear the centre props before the CTA tile grows. Otherwise sinking boxes and shrinking pawns show as specks under the tile.
