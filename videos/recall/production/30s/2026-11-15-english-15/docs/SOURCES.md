# Sources and claim check — Recall english-15 "Where's this water from?" (why a cold drink sweats)

Sources were read on 2026-11-15 as page text in headless Chromium. Quotes are verbatim.

## Topic choice
The batch suggested "Why a microwave heats your food but the plate feels different" (dielectric heating of water and other
polar/ionic constituents; dish heating and conduction; the "inside out" misconception). That subject is already covered by
**E11** (2026-11-09, context 25: "Why is the food steaming but the plate still cool?" — dielectric heating of water vs
conduction into the plate; pottery that heats itself), and the "outer layers first / not inside out" point by **R02**
(context 16). So a genuinely different everyday-science story with an equally strong first frame was chosen: **why a cold
drink "sweats"** — the water on the outside comes from the air, not the glass. Condensation was never explained in an earlier
episode (H03 used an iced glass only as a prop).

## Verbatim quotes
- **S1:** "As the temperature of water vapor (a gas) decreases, it will reach the point at which it turns into a liquid (called the dew point, or the point at which dew forms)." "Ask the students where the water on the outside of the glass came from." "As air comes in contact with the glass, it is cooled to below the dew point temperature, and the water vapor contained in the air condenses onto the glass." "The amount of water on the side of the glass depends upon the humidity, which is a measurement of how saturated with water vapor the air is." "The greater the moisture, the greater the water that can condense."
- **S2:** "By definition, the dew point is the approximate temperature to which the water vapor (the gaseous form of water) in the air must be cooled (at constant pressure) in order for it to condense into liquid water drops." "…the dew point temperature is also an absolute measure of the amount of water vapor in the air." "Molecules of water in the gas phase (water vapor) are zinging around in the air, but when a water vapor molecule strikes an object (like the side of the glass), it may "stick" (that is, condense on the surface)." "…we always have a rate of condensation and a rate of evaporation for every surface."
- **S3:** "Dew drops like these … form when the air temperature drops to or below the dew point." "At the dew point, the rate at which water molecules join together is greater than the rate at which they separate, and some of the water condenses to form droplets." "In everyday language, people sometimes refer to the capacity of air to "hold" water vapor, but this is not actually what happens. The water vapor is not held by the air."
