## Topic check → topic changed (why)
The suggested topic, "Why induction cooks a pan but not the glass directly" (alternating magnetic field, induced currents in a
compatible pan, resistance heating, glass warmed by the pan), is already covered by **H04** (2026-11-04, context 18,
`production/30s/2026-11-04-hindi-04/`): a copper coil makes a rapidly changing magnetic field, eddy currents form in the iron base
so the pot heats itself, and aluminium or glass vessels often don't heat (magnet test). An English remake would repeat the same
mechanism, visual and takeaway. Per the brief's rule, a **genuinely different kitchen-physics topic in the same genre** was chosen:
**why a pressure cooker cooks faster** (the boiling point rises with pressure; the reverse happens up a mountain). No earlier
episode covers boiling points, pressure or altitude.

## Verbatim quotes
- **S1:** "For example, the boiling point of water is 100ºC at 1.00 atm. As the pressure increases, the boiling temperature rises steadily to 374ºC at a pressure of 218 atm. A pressure cooker (or even a covered pot) will cook food faster because the water can exist as a liquid at temperatures greater than 100ºC without all boiling away."
- **S2:** Table 13.5: 100 °C — 1.01×10⁵ Pa; 120 °C — 1.99×10⁵ Pa. "You will note from Table 13.5 that the vapor pressure of water at 100ºC is 1.01×10⁵ Pa, or 1.00 atm. Thus, it can evaporate without limit at this temperature and pressure." "The bubble grows in size and thereby increases the buoyant force. The bubble breaks away and rises rapidly to the surface—we call this boiling!"
- **S3:** "Both stovetop and electric pressure cookers work based on the concept that water boils hotter under pressure, which allows food to cook faster." "When steam remains in the unit under pressure, the temperature inside the saucepan can go above the normal boiling point of water (212°F). For example, at 15 pounds of pressure, the temperature inside the pressure cooker can reach 250°F."
- **S4:** "As atmospheric pressure decreases, water boils at lower temperatures. At sea level, it boils at 212°F, while each 500-ft increase in altitude causes a drop of about 1°F in the boiling point of water." "At very high altitudes, more time is required for food to reach the desired internal cooking temperature." "Vegetables, legumes, pot roasts, soups, stews, and other foods cooked with moisture at 5,000 ft elevation or above require additional cooking time."
